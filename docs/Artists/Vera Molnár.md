# Vera Molnár (1924–2023)

<figure markdown>
  ![Vera Molnár in 1996](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a1/Vera_Moln%C3%A1r_%281996%29.png){ width=300 }
  <figcaption>Vera Molnár in 1996. Photo by Pantalaskas, [CC BY-SA 3.0](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Vera_Moln%C3%A1r_(1996).png).</figcaption>
</figure>

Hungarian-born French artist, pioneer of computer-generated and generative art.
One of the first women to use computers in fine art practice. Lived and worked
in Paris from 1947 until her death in December 2023, a month short of her
hundredth birthday.[1][4][9]

## Biography

### Early life and education

Vera Gács was born on 5 January 1924 in Budapest, the only child of a
middle-class secular Jewish family.[2] She studied art history and aesthetics
at the Hungarian Academy of Fine Arts from 1942 to 1947, one of only two
Jewish students admitted under the anti-Semitic climate of the time.[2] She
created her first non-representational, geometrically determined paintings
around 1946.[1]

In 1947 she received a scholarship to study in Rome at the Villa Giulia, then
moved to Paris that same year.[1] She married François Molnár (1922–1993), a
scientist who became a perceptual psychologist, in 1948.[1] François studied
how the eye scans a picture, and the two treated painting as an experiment on
the viewer throughout his life.[4]

### Paris and the move toward systems

In Paris, Molnár gravitated toward "cold" abstraction — lines drawn with
rulers and protractors, devoid of expressionist feeling.[2] She was inspired
by Mondrian, Malevich, and the concrete art movement.[5][6] The painter
Sonia Delaunay, whom she met in 1947, was an early mentor and encouraged her
to persist.[5]

Around 1960, she and François co-founded the **Groupe de Recherche d'Art
Visuel (GRAV)** with François Morellet, Julio Le Parc, and others — a
collective committed to art made from systems, optics, and movement rather
than personal expression.[1][4] In 1967, she co-founded a second group,
**Art et Informatique**, focused on art and computing.[1][2] Through this
group she met the composer Pierre Barbaud, who gave her access to a mainframe
computer at the French manufacturer Bull in 1968.[2]

### Computer access and plotter drawings

In 1968, Molnár gained access to a computer at a research centre in Paris
with a Benson plotter attached, and learned FORTRAN and then BASIC to drive
it.[4] Throughout the 1970s she worked on an IBM mainframe at the Centre
Inter-régional de Calcul Éléctronique (CIRCE) in a Paris suburb, generating
thousands of plotter drawings.[2] In 1974, a computer with a screen became
available, enabling her to examine results immediately — she described this
as a rebirth: *"I was born a second time with the visualization of the
screen."*[5]

### Later life and recognition

Recognition came late, as it typically does for women in the history of
technology.[4] From 1990 her work entered major European surveys. She
received the DAM Digital Art Award in 2005, was made Chevalier of Arts and
Letters in 2007, and won the AWARE Prix d'Honneur in 2018.[1][4] In the
2010s, MoMA, the Tate, and the Centre Pompidou began acquiring her work.[4]

In 2022, at age 98, she was exhibited at the **59th Venice Biennale**.[1][4]
In July 2023, her algorithmic series *Themes and Variations* was issued as
generative NFTs through Sotheby's, selling for approximately $1.2 million;
she treated the blockchain as "one more plotter."[4]

She died on 7 December 2023 in Paris.[1] The Centre Pompidou retrospective
**Parler à l'œil** (Speaking to the Eye), planned for her centenary, opened
on 28 February 2024 and ran through 26 August 2024.[4][8]

## The *machine imaginaire*

The concept at the heart of Molnár's practice is the *machine imaginaire*
(imaginary machine), developed around 1959–60.[2][4] More of a method than
an actual machine, it was an early example of generative art: the artist
executed "algorithms by hand," following step-by-step instructions to create
series of drawings and collages exploring variations on geometric forms.[2]

A picture began as a rule — a grid of squares to be rotated, or a set of
lines to be displaced. The rule was executed by hand, step by step, with
dice or tables of random numbers (sometimes pointing to digits in a
telephone book) deciding what the rule left open, across a whole series of
sheets.[2][4] She was the computer — "a slow and fallible one," as one
source puts it.[4] The point was to remove the artist's taste from
individual decisions, see what the rule produced, then judge, adjust, and
run again.[4]

When she gained access to a real computer in 1968, she simply handed it the
rules she had been executing by hand.[4] The name *machine imaginaire* came
from her friend Michel Philippot (1925–1996), a composer exploring serial
approaches to music.[2]

## Key works and series

### *Interruptions* (1968–69)

<figure markdown>
  ![Interruptions à recouvrements (1969) — computer-generated line art](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c1/Vera_Molnar._Interruptions_%C3%A0_recouvrements%E2%80%9D_from_1969.jpg){ width=500 }
  <figcaption>*Interruptions à recouvrements*, 1969. Computer-generated line art. [CC0](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Vera_Molnar._Interruptions_%C3%A0_recouvrements%E2%80%9D_from_1969.jpg) — Spalter Digital Art Collection.</figcaption>
</figure>

Fields of short lines in which the orientation varies slightly and a few are
missing — the untouched areas creating "interruptions" of white space.[4][6]
These are among her earliest computer-generated images and plotter
drawings, produced using a computer program of her own devising.[6]

**View the original at:**

- [V&A, London (E.269-2011)](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1193775/interruptions-drawing-molnar-vera/) — plotter drawing, 1969
- [National Gallery of Art, Washington D.C.](https://www.nga.gov/artworks/216160-interruptions) — computer drawing in black ink on Benson plotter paper
- [The Morgan Library & Museum](https://www.themorgan.org/drawings/item/405692) — created with a Benson plotter, black ink, 1968

### *(Dés)Ordres* (*Disorder* / *Structure of Squares*, 1974)

Concentric squares repeated with small random perturbations — "1% disorder"
introduced into a regular grid.[4][3] The V&A collection includes both
*(Dés)Ordres* and *Structure of Squares* (1974, plotter drawings).[3]

**View the original at:**

- [V&A, London — *(Dés)Ordres* (E.271-2011)](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1193781/desordres-drawing-molnar-vera/) — 20×20 square grid, 1974
- [V&A, London — *Structure of Squares* (E.270-2011)](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1193777/structures-of-squares-drawing-vera-molnar/) — plotter drawing, 1974

### *Hommage à Barbaud* (1974)

Plotter drawings dedicated to composer Pierre Barbaud, who facilitated her
first computer access. These works exhibit entropic progression from order
to disorder.[2]

### *Transformations* (1976)

Algorithmic morphing of line segments — a form repeated with progressive
random disruption. Her first solo exhibition, also titled *Transformations*,
was held in 1976 at the gallery of the London Polytechnic.[1][6]

**View related works at:**

- [MoMA — *Molndrian* (1974)](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/417832) — plotter-drawn ink on paper
- [MoMA — *2 Letter Ms (From the Cycle M As in Malevich)* (1961)](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/409640) — cut-and-pasted colored paper on board

### *Molnart* (1974–76)

A program written with François Molnár that let her vary drawing parameters
interactively and see results immediately — the ability to "make a thousand
versions in an afternoon and keep the few that surprised her."[4]

### *Lettres de ma mère* (*My Mother's Letters*, 1981–91)

A deeply personal series simulating her late mother Erzsi's handwriting
through computer-generated asemic marks — lines that look like writing but
carry no semantic meaning.[2] Molnár began with hand-drawn zigzag sketches
in 1981, then translated them into computer code in 1984, outputting
plotter drawings on long scrolls of paper.[2] The lines become
"more and more chaotic as they advance to the right" through an increasingly
random process.[2] She later overlaid the computer output with her own
freehand marks (*contre-écritures*), creating a dialogue between hand and
machine.[2]

### *2% de désordre en co-opération* (2022)

An NFT series created for her 98th birthday: a 100-square grid where a
studio visitor colors one square, Molnár fills a second in black, and the
remaining 98 stay empty — "2% disorder in cooperation."[6]

## Working method and philosophy

Molnár's approach was distinct from other computer art pioneers. In a 1975
text for *Leonardo*, she wrote: *"Whereas they begin with an initial set of
rules (a grammar) specifying the way parameters are to be varied, I try to
elaborate the rules as a work develops."*[2] Rarely did she set out with a
preconceived plan; rather, she tinkered with programs and parameters until
reaching a desired outcome. This reassertion of subjective choice was a
necessary part of her process — there were certain artistic decisions the
computer simply could not make.[2]

Her working motto was "99% order, 1% disorder" — a small, controlled amount
of randomness introduced into geometric regularity to see what it does.[4]
She described her practice as "putting surprise into monotony and symmetry,"
adding: "leave a little air in. As in politics, we've seen where orthodoxies
lead."[4]

She framed the computer not as a replacement for the artist but as a
conversation partner. "It may seem paradoxical," she wrote in 1989, "that
this so-called inhuman machine helps to realize what is most subjective,
what is most profound in man."[2]

She also credited two machines with enabling her career: "the computer and
the dishwashing machine" — the latter freeing her from domestic labor to
focus on art.[5]

## Influence and legacy

Molnár's compositions are a popular subject in creative coding communities.
The **Recode Project** preserves computer art by translating it into modern
languages like Processing.[2] Zach Lieberman's MIT course *Recreating the
Past* routinely begins with an assignment to reprogram a Molnár.[2]

Her work is held in major collections including MoMA (New York), Tate
(London), Centre Pompidou (Paris), V&A (London), National Gallery of Art
(Washington D.C.), Museum of Fine Arts Houston, and LACMA.[1]

Art historian Zsofi Valyi-Nagy, who studied Molnár's process through media
archaeology, observed: "familiar viewers will recognize Molnár's visual
language: her geometric forms, her variations on a theme, and her
minimalistic lines, which, whether drawn by hand or with a plotter, are
unmistakably hers."[3]

Molnár said she had no regrets: "My life was squares, triangles and
lines."[4]
